Model Evaluation

This page shows how well the face-recognition system actually works. We tested it on two sets of photos. The first is our own custom set, taken on purpose under difficult conditions: masks, hats, sunglasses, side views, poor lighting. The second is LFW, a well-known public set of clean, front-facing photos used by researchers as a reference.

The goal is not to pick a winner — the two sets are very different. LFW tells us the system is built correctly; the custom set tells us how it copes with real, messy conditions. The difference between the two is the interesting part.

Summary

A few standard numbers. EER is the error rate when false accepts and false rejects are balanced (lower is better). AUC measures overall separation (1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is random). d' says how far apart the "same person" and "different person" scores are (higher is better). The ranges in brackets are 95% confidence intervals — how much the number could move given the sample size.

Score distributions

Every comparison produces a similarity score. Blue bars are real matches (same person), orange bars are impostors (different people). When the two colours are well separated, the system can tell them apart easily. On the custom set (left) they overlap a lot — that is why it struggles. On LFW (right) they are clearly separated.

ROC curves

This curve shows the trade-off between accepting the right people and wrongly accepting impostors. The closer the line hugs the top-left corner, the better. LFW (orange) climbs almost to the corner; the custom curve (blue) stays lower. The custom line looks like a staircase simply because it was tested on only a few impostors, so it can move in big steps.

Results by photo condition

For the custom set, this breaks every photo into three outcomes: correctly recognised (green), wrongly handled (orange), or discarded before matching because the face was too hidden or turned away (red). Normal and glasses photos are almost all correct; masks and side views cause most of the errors and rejections.

Discarded photos

24 photos were set aside before any matching. Most (21) were turned away by a deliberate rule that refuses faces seen too much from the side — aligning them would be unreliable. The rest (3) had no face detected at all, usually because a mask or hat hid the key facial points. This is by design, not a malfunction.

Confusion matrix

Each row is the true person, each column is who the system guessed. The dark diagonal means most people are recognised correctly. The off-diagonal cells show mix-ups — pairs of people whose faces the system finds similar. These are the natural targets for improvement (more enrolment photos, or a smarter classifier).

Note: the custom dataset is small (10 people). Results give a clear trend but the exact numbers carry wide uncertainty, as the confidence intervals above show.
